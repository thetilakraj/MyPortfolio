The most useful thing enterprise work gave me wasn't a technology. It was a
default I didn't know I'd absorbed until I caught myself applying it a decade
later, to a product nobody was making me build that way.

_No client names or system detail. The enterprise half of this is described as
practice._

## The first time

Around 2016 I built offline-capable SAP mobile applications using the SMP SDK
with Cordova and Kapsel plugins. This was not a fashionable architecture. It
was a requirement, and it came from the users.

The people using these apps worked in plants and in the field. Connectivity
there was not a spectrum from fast to slow — it was a binary that flipped
without warning, in places where the work continued regardless. An app that
assumed a network was not a degraded app. It was an app that got left on the
truck.

That forces a particular discipline. The device has to hold enough state to be
useful alone. Sync becomes an explicit, designed event rather than an
invisible assumption. Conflict resolution has to have an answer before it has a
conflict. And crucially, the interface has to tell the truth about what it
knows: which records are local, which are confirmed, and what will happen when
signal returns.

Offline was tedious to build and it was never the part of the demo anyone
applauded. It was the part that determined whether the rollout succeeded.

## The second time

In 2025 I started building a spelling learning app for adults and ESL learners.
Nobody required offline. The obvious architecture for a modern iOS app is a
thin client with a server holding the learning model — easier to iterate,
easier to instrument, easier to change your mind about.

I chose offline-first anyway, and it took me a while to articulate why beyond
"it felt right."

The honest reason is the same one from the plant floor. The learners I care
about practise in transit — on a bus, on a train, in the twenty minutes between
things. That is exactly where connectivity is worst and exactly where the habit
either forms or doesn't. An app that needs a network in those twenty minutes
is an app that gets deleted in week three.

The consequence is the same discipline, arrived at from the opposite
direction: every learning decision has to be computable on-device from
persisted state. Progress, evidence, and session composition all resolve
locally. Nothing waits on a round trip to decide what the learner sees next.

## What actually transferred

Not the SDK — none of that code or tooling is relevant to Swift. What
transferred was three habits.

**State is the product.** In an offline-first system you cannot hide behind the
server as the source of truth. Whatever the device persists *is* the truth
until proven otherwise, which forces you to be precise about what you're
storing and why. Both systems end up with an explicit, versioned local schema
because there is nowhere else to put the ambiguity.

**Failure states are designed, not caught.** Offline work teaches you that the
degraded path is a real path with real users on it, so it gets designed with
the same care as the happy one. That habit is why the radio product I built has
an honest offline shell rather than a spinner, and why a bad practice session
still closes on something the learner can do.

**Atomicity or nothing.** Interruption is normal — a tunnel, a phone call, a
force-quit. Both systems persist state and feedback together, before advancing,
because the alternative is a learner or a technician discovering later that the
last thing they did didn't count.

## Why it matters here

Enterprise work is often framed as the thing you do before you get to do
interesting work. That framing gets the direction of causation backwards.

The constraint I was handed in 2016 — assume the network is gone — turned out
to be a better product instinct than anything I would have arrived at with
total freedom. When I finally had that freedom, I used it to impose the same
constraint on myself.

## What I'd change

**I'd have named the pattern sooner.** I carried this as an instinct for nearly
a decade before writing it down as a principle. Instincts don't transfer to
other people, and they don't survive a busy quarter. Written constraints do —
which is why both of my own products now start with the constraints in a
document before anything is built.
