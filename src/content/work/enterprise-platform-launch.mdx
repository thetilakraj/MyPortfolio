Since September 2024 I've been technical lead on the delivery of an enterprise
SAP BTP platform toward production launch, working across multiple vendor
teams.

_This case study describes how the delivery is run, not what the system
contains. The platform is internal, so there are no screenshots, no
architecture detail, and no product name here. What follows is the part that
transfers anyway: the practice._

## Situation

I joined the organisation in 2022 as a senior software developer and moved into
the technical lead role in 2024, as the programme shifted from building toward
launching.

That transition changes the job completely. Writing the code is no longer the
constraint. The constraints become whether work delivered by parties you don't
manage meets a standard you're accountable for, whether the environment it
lands in is actually ready, and whether anyone can tell the difference before
production does.

## Constraints

**Multi-vendor delivery.** Significant development is performed by external
vendors. I review it and I'm accountable for it, but I don't own the teams
producing it — influence has to run through standards, review, and evidence
rather than direct authority.

**Multi-party environment.** Developers, system analysts, and subject-matter
experts across several organisations, each with their own delivery cadence and
their own definition of done.

**A launch date meets a live business.** Post-launch defects are not tickets in
a backlog; they're an operational problem for the people who depend on the
platform. Severity triage has to be agreed before it's needed, not during the
first incident.

**Breadth over depth, deliberately.** QA responsibility spans all business
domains across both the BTP layer and the Gateway services. No single person
can hold deep domain expertise across all of it, so the coverage strategy has
to compensate for that rather than pretend otherwise.

## Decisions

**Standards before review, review before merge.** Code review across vendor
SAPUI5 and CAP work is only leverage if the standard exists in writing first.
Reviewing against an unstated preference produces argument; reviewing against
an agreed coding standard produces a fix. Best practices and coding standards
are applied through daily stand-ups with the application vendors and a weekly
technical meeting with the services vendors — separate cadences, because the
integration questions and the service questions fail differently.

**Architecture alignment is reviewed, not assumed.** Vendor architecture and
technology stack decisions get reviewed against the target explicitly, early,
rather than being discovered at integration. The same applies to BTP setup,
identity and security configuration, and repository practice — each is a
review surface with a named owner rather than an assumption.

**One implementation plan covering the unglamorous parts.** Pre-implementation
activities, the implementation itself, smoke test, and post-implementation
support are planned as one artifact. Most launch pain I've seen comes from
treating the first and last of those as someone else's problem.

**Write the specification down.** Authoring the Gateway and ECC technical
specification documents is not administrative overhead — it's the mechanism by
which a multi-party team agrees what "correct" means before anyone builds
against it.

**Risks get named while they're cheap.** Reviewing vendor setup across BTP,
security, identity and version control surfaces risk while it's still a
conversation instead of an incident.

## What shipped

Operational readiness ownership for the platform, and technical lead on the
production launch.

Production environment setup and configuration. An implementation plan spanning
pre-implementation through post-launch support, with severity-based resolution
timelines agreed in advance. Vendor code review across SAPUI5 and CAP.
Architecture and stack alignment review with vendors. QA across all business
domains for both BTP and Gateway services, with high and medium defects
resolved inside the planned window. Technical specification authorship for
Gateway and ECC. Review of vendor BTP setup, identity and security
configuration, and repository practice. Ongoing production releases, Jira
delivery, and after-hours support.

## Outcome

The programme is in flight, so the honest status is in-progress rather than
delivered. Launch readiness is the current work.

What I can say concretely: the review cadence catches integration problems at
review rather than at integration, and the specification documents have
repeatedly turned "we assumed you meant" conversations into short ones. Those
are process outcomes rather than headline metrics, which is what this stage of
a programme actually produces.

## What I'd change

**I set up the review cadence later than I should have.** The daily and weekly
rhythms with the two vendor groups now do most of the quality work, and every
week they didn't exist was a week defects travelled further before being seen.
On a future programme that's a week-one artifact, not a response to friction.

**Breadth-first QA needs tooling earlier.** Owning QA across every business
domain is manageable through discipline for a while and then abruptly isn't.
Regression coverage should scale with domain count from the start rather than
being reinforced once manual coverage strains.

**Specifications should be versioned like code.** They're treated as
deliverables and they behave like source — they drift, they need review, and
disagreement about which version is current is expensive. I'd put them under
the same review discipline as the code they govern.
