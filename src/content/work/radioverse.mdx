Radioverse is a live internet radio app built on one deliberate inversion: the
**station** is the primary object, not the track. Everything else — the
catalogue, the player, the recovery behaviour — follows from that.

## Situation

Internet radio directories are abundant and almost all of them are bad in the
same way. They are exhaustive rather than curated, so a search returns
thousands of results of which a large share are dead streams, duplicates of
each other, or mislabelled talk stations sitting under a music genre. The
listener does the quality control, one broken stream at a time.

The opposite failure is the streaming-service model, where the track is the
object and stations are just playlists with worse metadata. That throws away
the thing radio is actually good at: someone, somewhere, choosing what plays
next.

I wanted the second thing without the first thing's tax. Curated music-first
discovery, no ads, and a player that behaves like an object rather than a
web page.

## Constraints

**Solo, across every layer.** Research, design system, frontend, backend,
billing, security, and production operations. There is no one to hand a spec
to, which sounds like freedom and is mostly a forcing function — anything I
build badly, I also maintain badly.

**Live audio is not fakeable.** A streaming app cannot pretend to work offline,
and the honest failure states are a real design surface rather than an
afterthought. Streams stall mid-song, go dead without warning, and vary wildly
in startup latency depending on network quality.

**The source data is hostile.** The catalogue is assembled from third-party
directories — Radio Browser primary, IPRD secondary — which means duplicates,
dead endpoints, inconsistent genre taxonomies, and heavy country skew are the
input condition, not an edge case.

**Real money.** Billing is live, so gating has to be enforced server-side and
the free tier has to stay genuinely usable rather than a nag screen.

## Decisions

**Catalogue as an editorial product, not a query.** Rather than proxying a
directory, the app builds a curated base catalogue: country quotas across 34
supported markets inside a fixed headroom limit, quality filtering, dedupe,
and genre balancing, with an editorially curated dataset that is authoritative
for its own entries and overrides the upstream sources. Firestore holds a
fallback snapshot so an upstream outage degrades instead of failing.

The rejected alternative was live-querying the directory per search. Cheaper to
build, and it would have made the catalogue's quality permanently someone
else's problem.

**Resilience as a feature, not error handling.** Playback carries mid-stream
stall recovery behind a single-deadline rebuffer watchdog, startup timeouts
scaled to measured network quality, and a session-scoped memory of stations
that already failed so Shuffle stops offering them. Previous walks real history
rather than re-randomising, and Next re-walks forward through it.

**A runtime quality engine that is allowed to say "I don't know."** Playback
emits privacy-safe quality events, aggregated daily into compact snapshots that
feed bounded ranking, shuffle, and fallback hints. Every hint is
confidence-tiered. In production, event ingestion, ranking hints, and probes
are enabled while fallback influence stays off.

This is the decision I'd defend hardest and the one that currently looks least
impressive — see the last section.

**One locked visual direction.** The system is committed to a restrained
physical audio instrument: a matte charcoal shell, a plinth as the primary
object, the vinyl seated inside it. Every surface holds the same material
ratio.

> 80% black shadow depth, 15% white and metal edge highlight, 5% amber signal.
>
> Amber is signal-only — active station, primary playback state, premium
> markers, tiny hardware LEDs. It never becomes decoration.

Writing that ratio down converted a hundred future style arguments into one
lookup. It is also the rule this portfolio borrows.

## What shipped

Free, after login: browse and search live stations by station, country, genre
and language; play with next, previous, shuffle, mute and volume; an immersive
fullscreen player; favourites; track likes and a lightweight listening history;
and installation as a PWA with an offline shell that never pretends live audio
works offline.

Premium, on live Stripe billing: on-demand song identification from the audio
of the currently playing station, server-side gated and daily-capped; playlists
that keep provenance back to the station the track came from; a sleep timer
with a gentle fade; a floating desktop player; and flag-gated device-local EQ
presets and genre-aware immersive themes.

Underneath: an App Router PWA with Media Session controls and a Serwist service
worker holding strict network-only rules for identify, auth, Firebase and media
paths; production security headers, a report-only CSP, explicit API method
guards, tightened input validation on identify, service-worker notification URL
constraints, Firestore rule regression coverage, and client-asset secret
scanning.

## Outcome

Live in production and paying its own way, built and operated by one person.

The performance numbers above are the two that moved most. Mobile first load
was the worst experience in the product — a cold container plus an
unconstrained catalogue response plus a player whose height wasn't reserved
until audio metadata resolved. Fixing it took three unglamorous changes:
guaranteeing a warm instance, negotiating gzip on the catalogue endpoint, and
reserving the console's layout box up front.

I am deliberately not quoting a user count. The product is early and the number
would invite the wrong comparison — the claim here is that one person shipped a
billing-enabled, security-hardened, resilient streaming PWA, and that claim
stands on the engineering.

## What I'd change

**I built the quality engine ahead of its data.** It is correct, it is
privacy-safe, and at current traffic its confidence tiers stay low enough that
ranking is effectively neutral. The architecture is sound and the investment
was premature; the same effort spent on discovery surfaces would have produced
visible value sooner. I'd build the event pipeline first and the ranking layer
only once the volume justified it.

**Performance is a release gate, not a solved problem.** The improvements above
were measured locally and in targeted production checks. A full production
mobile Lighthouse remeasure is still open, and I'd rather say that than imply a
clean bill of health.

**Re-engagement is scaffolding, honestly labelled.** Share Target and App
Badging are implemented, token registration is guarded, and the visible opt-in
rail is hidden for this release. There are no push jobs or schedulers running.
It's a foundation, and calling it a shipped feature would be a lie I'd have to
maintain.
