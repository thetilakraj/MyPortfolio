Six years, three employers, and something close to sixty applications delivered
end to end in SAP ECC and S/4HANA landscapes for clients in energy
(hydrocarbon), retail, and FMCG.

_Client work lives in client systems. This describes the patterns and the
practice — no client names, no screenshots, no system detail._

## Situation

Enterprise SAP interfaces have a deserved reputation. The default is a
transaction screen designed for someone who was trained on it for a week, has
used it daily for nine years, and would rather you changed nothing. Fiori was
SAP's answer to that, and rolling it out across an existing landscape means
retrofitting a design language onto processes that predate it by decades.

The work is rarely about inventing an interaction. It's about finding the
smallest coherent surface that a business process can be squeezed into without
lying about the process.

## Constraints

**A design language you didn't choose.** Fiori is a system with opinions.
Deviating is possible, expensive, and usually a mistake — an app that looks
unlike the launchpad around it reads as broken rather than as bespoke.

**The backend is the immovable object.** Data models, gateway services, and
authorisation objects are typically fixed before design starts. The interface
has to absorb the mismatch between how the business describes its work and how
the system stores it.

**Users you don't get much access to.** Enterprise users are doing their actual
jobs. Research happens in scheduled focus groups, not on demand, so each
session has to earn its cost.

**Multi-language and multi-region by default.** Layouts that only survive
English are not finished layouts.

## Decisions

**Design in the client's language, validate in mine.** UX work ran through
client focus groups with prototypes built in SAP Build and Figma. Showing a
process owner a clickable flow surfaces disagreements that a specification
review never will — most usefully, disagreements between two people on the
client side who both thought the process was obvious.

**Find the archetype, then reuse it.** Across employers the same shapes recur:
an analytical dashboard over a domain, a hierarchical table for structures that
are genuinely trees, a paired create-and-approve workflow, a master data
governance surface with a multi-stage approval matrix, a document or attachment
service, a scan-driven entry point.

The most useful thing I learned in this period is that the second app in a
family is a design opportunity and the ninth is a systems problem. On a suite
of paired request-and-administration applications, treating them as one
interaction system with nine instantiations — rather than nine apps that
resemble each other — was the difference between a consistent product and nine
slightly different opinions about what a date picker is.

**Notifications are part of the interface.** A surprising share of enterprise
UX lives in the email a user receives when something needs them, not in the app
they open afterwards. Treating notification templates as a designed surface
rather than a developer afterthought reliably reduced the "where do I click"
support load.

**Offline is an architecture decision, not a setting.** For field and plant
users, offline-capable mobile via the SMP SDK with Cordova and Kapsel plugins
was the difference between an app that got used and an app that got blamed.
That thread is picked up separately in *Offline-first, twice*.

## What shipped

Analytical and portfolio dashboards with drill-down and custom filtering.
Hierarchical data tables. Sales and procurement transaction applications.
Process flows, organisational charts, and document services. Barcode-driven
scanning and ordering. Offline-capable mobile applications.

Master data governance surfaces with multi-stage approval matrices, cost centre
and internal order management, master data request monitoring, and the
notification systems around them.

A suite of paired request and administration applications spanning expense,
procurement, and permitting — one interaction system, nine deployments.

## Outcome

The work carried a client through to a Diamond Client relationship and earned
an Accenture Excellence Award, which is the only externally validated outcome I
can point at from this period without disclosing client detail.

The more durable outcome is the pattern library in my head. Nearly every
product decision I make now — the paired create/approve shape, treating
notifications as interface, refusing to let the ninth instance drift — traces
back to something that went wrong once in an enterprise rollout.

## What I'd change

**I designed screens for too long before designing systems.** For the first two
or three years I optimised individual applications. The leverage was always one
level up, in the consistency between them, and I found that late.

**I under-invested in research access.** Focus groups were scheduled because
that was the process. I never pushed hard enough for shorter, more frequent
contact with actual end users, and I shipped things a fifteen-minute
conversation would have prevented.

**I treated documentation as an output rather than a design tool.** Writing the
specification first turns out to be the cheapest way to discover that two
stakeholders disagree. I learned that as a lead, and I'd have been more useful
sooner if I'd learned it as a consultant.
