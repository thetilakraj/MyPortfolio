Spellum teaches reusable spelling **patterns** and then checks whether they
transferred to words the learner has never seen. That single measurement choice
determines nearly everything else in the product.

## Situation

Spelling apps overwhelmingly measure the wrong thing. They count words
completed, award streaks for showing up, and let a learner grind a familiar
list to a high score without ever demonstrating they can spell something new.
The number goes up; the skill may not.

That's a comfortable design because completion volume is easy to measure, easy
to gamify, and correlates with engagement metrics. It's also why someone can
spend three months in an app and still freeze on an unfamiliar word.

The product question I wanted to answer: what does it look like if the app only
claims progress it can actually evidence?

## Constraints

**The honest metric is the hard one.** Measuring transfer means holding back
unseen words as probes, which costs session time that could have gone to
practice, and produces slower, less flattering progress curves.

**Offline-first, for real.** The learners I care about — adults and ESL
learners, often practising in transit — cannot depend on connectivity. Every
learning decision has to be computable on-device from persisted state.

**No claims without evidence.** If the app asserts "you've mastered this
pattern," that has to be traceable to reviewed content and observed learner
behaviour, not to a heuristic that happened to fire.

**Accessibility is a correctness constraint.** A spelling app that conveys
correctness through colour alone is broken for a meaningful share of its users.

## Decisions

**Written authority, checksum-verified.** Implementation follows a fixed
precedence chain: Product Knowledge Architecture, then the Core Learning Loop
UX Brief, then the design response, then SwiftUI. A verification script
checksums the authority documents. The design response resolves the brief
visually and is explicitly not allowed to change product scope or learning
policy.

This sounds heavy for a solo project. It is the reason the product still has a
coherent thesis after twelve build phases.

**Fail closed, everywhere.** Corpus schema v4 authorises calibration and
ordinary practice separately, and both refuse to run when their explicit
authoring flag is absent. Incomplete reviewed content leaves practice
unavailable rather than degrading into something unreviewed. Distribution
policy files keep release gated until reviewed evidence exists. Debug builds
expose deterministic screen fixtures; TestFlight and App Store builds never
ship them.

**Evidence accumulates before it speaks.** Diagnostic relationships need to be
reviewed and uniquely mapped before they generate evidence at all, and then
confirm only on a second independent observation. Missing or ambiguous mappings
stay unclassified rather than guessing. A pattern insight is only offered when
two distinct words from the learner's own history plus a reviewed authored
explanation support it.

**Endings are designed, not defaulted.** Every session persists an unused
high-confidence final item plus deterministic alternatives, so a session that
goes badly at the end can still close on something the learner can do. When
those are exhausted, the app shows a calm fatigue ending rather than failure
framing.

**Colour never carries correctness alone.** Corrections use amber *plus*
alignment, a label, an underline, and a weight change. Progress is proportional
and carries an approximate spoken label — never a score, never a numeric
accessibility value.

> Authored OKLCH colour roles compile once into Display P3 asset catalogs.
> SwiftUI consumes semantic theme roles only; feature code contains no palette
> literals.

The typography is split three ways by job: a system serif for editorial voice,
SF Pro for instruction and interface, SF Mono for spelling strings and
graphemes. A readable-typography preference adds tracking and line spacing —
and deliberately makes no claim that spacing or any typeface treats dyslexia.

## What shipped

Currently on internal TestFlight, not yet publicly available.

The production slice runs end to end: first-run preferences and calibration
resume through the real orchestrator; Home composes and persists an explicitly
authorised practice session, verifies offline system speech, and routes into
it. Responses persist feedback and learner state atomically before advancing.
Reviewed unseen-word probes stay marked as transfer items in the persisted
plan, and a first correct transfer earns one durable milestone whose
acknowledgement survives relaunch.

Sessions close on an authored summary that reuses the session's pattern moment,
shows at most two eligible confidence changes, preserves transfer proof, and
requires an explicit acknowledgement before another session can start. Free
learners get one session per local day, derived from persisted completion
state; the next strictly later local day reopens practice.

A "Spelling DNA" view derives from persisted transfer attempts and confirmed
diagnostic evidence — an aggregate preview for free learners, pattern-family
and error-share detail with Core. Unscored, ambiguous, or unreviewed evidence
stays invisible rather than becoming a learner-facing claim.

## Outcome

Too early for outcome claims, and I'd rather say that than manufacture one.

What exists is a product whose architecture matches its thesis: there is no
code path that lets Spellum tell a learner they've mastered something it hasn't
observed them do. That was the goal, and it's verifiable by reading the
composition root rather than by trusting a screenshot.

## What I'd change

**The authority chain slowed the first three phases considerably.** Writing the
product architecture, the UX brief, and the design response before meaningful
SwiftUI existed felt like procrastination at the time. It paid back later —
but I'd compress the first pass and let the documents earn their detail
incrementally rather than trying to specify twelve phases up front.

**Reviewed-content gating is a real bottleneck.** Failing closed on unreviewed
content is correct, and it means content authoring is now the critical path for
release rather than engineering. I under-estimated that when designing the
gates, and I'd build the authoring and review tooling in parallel with the
engine rather than after it.

**Remaining gates are open and named.** Reviewed content, Apple system
requirements, legal, device coverage, and operational readiness are all
explicitly unresolved. That's why there's no link on this page.
